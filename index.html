    Between April and September 2026 we ran 26 Movement Mondays sessions across three strands: Move Strong – Play Wild for families, Nature Moves for people aged 50 and over, and Bushcraft, which wasn't in the original plan and was added partway through because families asked for it.

    Thirteen people completed the feedback survey. Feeling welcomed and safe scored 5 out of 5 from every single respondent. Eleven of the thirteen said the sessions went beyond their expectations. Nine of the thirteen had never been to anything like this before.

    The numbers matter, but they aren't the story. The story is in the session logs.

    A boy who started the project whispering into his mum's ear so she could speak for him now checks the group out on his own, and his mum says it's showing up in other groups outside our sessions. Parents who watched from the side for weeks now join in — and every time one of them does, their child's engagement rises with them. A woman who arrived on a mobility scooter after ankle surgery is working towards walking her dog again, and came back one week buzzing because she'd used something we'd practised in a real situation. Another was walking her dog past the field on sticks after a hip replacement, joined in on the spot, and a month later put the sticks down to catch a bean bag.

    One thing we want to be straight about. The family strand is established and growing. The over-50s strand isn't where we want it — the groups have been small, and referral routes we expected to work haven't delivered. We've kept running it, we'd make that choice again, and we've learnt some specific things about why. That's all in the final section.

    About Movement Mondays

    One project, two programmes, and one thing we added because we listened

    Two programmes under one roof

    Nature Moves is for people aged 50 and over. Gentle outdoor movement, built around the things that make daily life easier — getting up off the floor, balancing, carrying, getting under and over things. It runs in Shotton, Deeside. It's free, and it's as much about the brew and the natter as the movement.

    Move Strong – Play Wild is for families. Same principles, different energy. Games, challenges and play that get children and adults moving together, outdoors, in all weathers.

    Both are invitational. People can take part, take it at their own pace, or sit at the side and watch until they're ready. Nobody is pushed. That matters more than it sounds — a good number of our regulars started out watching.

    Why bushcraft was added

    We didn't plan bushcraft at the start. The families told us they wanted it.

    Some children and adults connect through movement. Others connect through making something — a fire, a shelter, a carved stick. Once we saw that, we built it in. We now alternate between movement and bushcraft, and sometimes combine the two in a single session.

    That change came directly from listening, and it's been one of the most valuable decisions of the project. Several of our strongest moments this year happened around a fire or in a hammock, not in a game.

    How attendance moved across the six months

    AprMayJunJulAugSep
    Move Strong – Play Wild Bushcraft Nature Moves

    Two things stand out.

    August was the strongest month of the project. The school holidays let us run longer, fuller days — 3 August brought 16 people to a family session with 11 of them new, and 10 August brought 24 to a bushcraft day. Families came as families. One session had a three-week-old baby and a seventy-year-old in the same space.

    September held that gain rather than losing it. The first family session back after the holidays drew 24 people, our joint-highest of the period. The group that formed over the summer stayed together.

    Two strands, two different pictures

    Move Strong – Play Wild and Bushcraft have grown into a settled, well-attended community. Between them they account for 193 of the 214 attendances, and the shift between movement and bushcraft — the change families asked for — has kept people coming back rather than drifting off.

    Nature Moves has been a harder build. Groups have been small, sometimes one or two people, once just one. We've kept running regardless, and it's worth saying why: the smaller sessions have produced some of the most significant individual outcomes in the whole project. But the numbers are the numbers, and we cover what we've learnt in the final section.

    What changed for people

    Drawn from facilitator logs written after every session

    The logs were written in the moment, without knowing what would matter later. Read together across six months, four clear patterns emerge.

    People find their voice

    This came up more than any other theme.

    O started the project speaking only through his mum — whispering in her ear at check-in so she could say it for him. In June he checked in on his own, "excited". By July he took the talking stick first and checked the group out himself. In September he checked in with his big brother's support and checked out entirely alone.

    His mum told us this is now showing up outside our sessions, in other groups.

    He isn't the only one. In July a new boy who is selectively mute spoke to a facilitator on his first day. His mum said it was a credit to us that he chose to speak. In September, M — also selectively non-verbal — talked face to face about his new mountain bike toy, rather than through his dad.

    One parent wrote that her autistic son "isn't a very sociable person but he thrives in these sessions". Another told us that a year ago her son didn't speak to anyone, and that the space we've created helped him find his voice.

    "He doesn't speak to just anyone, so well done you."

    Parent, July

    Adults join in, and everything else improves

    We noticed early that when a parent takes part, their child's engagement changes.

    In April one mum who usually sat out took part at her own pace and surprised herself. Her son's engagement improved immediately — he took the chance to compete against her. At check-out, several of the younger children said they were happy to see her getting involved. She told us she'd decided to join in because her own mobility mattered to her.

    By May the mums were setting the tone for whole sessions. By September parents were among the most competitive people in the field.

    What makes it possible is permission to be bad at it. One mum wrote that she felt "free to be silly without judgement". A facilitator logged it as families being confident enough in the group to be silly and weird. That's the mechanism. People join in once it's safe to get it wrong.

    "It was nice to be able to move without thinking about it."

    Parent, May

    Movement changes when you stop thinking about it

    This runs right through Nature Moves.

    D came in saying she'd be rubbish at it, then set the best time in the group on the pick-up challenge. In August she moved noticeably better and faster the less she thought about it, and said afterwards: "I don't even know what I just did — I didn't think."

    P joined a session by chance. She was walking her dog past us, on walking sticks after a new hip, and her physiotherapist had told her to find a balance class. She joined in on the spot, dog included. Her arm went higher that day than it ever had — because she was playing rather than performing. In September she put her stick down entirely to catch bean bags.

    This is the argument for play as the delivery method. Games bypass the self-consciousness that stops people moving.

    "I learnt so much in an hour about my body and limitations."

    Participant, July

    Children who usually can't stay, stay

    Several children came to us with a pattern of disrupting and leaving.

    T was one. In July he became dysregulated, calmed himself, and came back to the group to finish well. The log notes that this would normally have been game over for him. The following week he didn't fully engage, but he didn't sabotage anything either — he took himself off with his mum while his sister carried on playing. The facilitator's note reads: week on week I see progress with this young man.

    In August, two boys came close to conflict, regulated themselves, and it passed within minutes. In July, two young people who usually distract others took part start to finish and supported others doing it. A late arrival who would normally unsettle the group didn't.

    The regulation games — Sleeping Giants in particular — do real work here. They lift energy and bring it back down, and the children have learnt to use them.

    What sits underneath all four

    Belonging. It shows up in the logs constantly and in the family feedback even more.

    A birthday boy chose to spend his birthday with us in August, and a regular brought a cake to share with everyone. New families are welcomed in by the existing group without prompting. A dad who works long hours found the summer sessions a way to spend proper time with his daughter. One mum came in with the Monday blues and left, in her words, feeling better for having played and got out of her head.

    "These sessions made us feel a massive sense of belonging. We felt included and respected and they're fun."

    Case studies

    Three people tracked across the six months. Initials used throughout.

    Nature Moves

    "You'd be proud of me this week"

    J came to her first session at the end of June.

    She'd had ankle surgery and lost all her confidence with it. She was getting around on a mobility scooter. Her physiotherapy had ended, but the confidence hadn't come back. It had been a hard year on other fronts too — illness, disability, the loss of her father, and caring for an elderly husband.

    She had one goal: to walk her dog again.

    That first session was balance and reaction work. The log from the day notes a few wobbles, but that she recovered well from each one, gave every task a go, and knew her own limits. Her confidence improved round by round.

    "Didn't think I could do that."

    J, first session, June

    She came back the following week with her husband. She chose the harder options rather than the easy ones, and the log records real skill in balance — pushing herself while still working at her own pace.

    The turning point came on 20 July. She was the only person who came that day. We checked in, warmed up, and worked the over-under obstacle course, a movement she'd been making steady progress on. Then we sat and had a brew before playing floor is lava, solving our way across the field around her obstacles and niggles.

    Before any of that, she told the facilitator he'd be proud of her. She'd used one of the movements we'd been working on, in a real situation, in her own life. She was buzzing and couldn't wait to say so.

    "You'd be proud of me this week!"

    J, July

    In August she came back while recovering from illness, rested when she needed to, and got stuck in anyway.

    Her written feedback was short: sessions are "having fun, while making balance and movement better", and what she's taking away is "knowing my balance is getting better".

    Why this mattersJ is exactly the person green social prescribing is meant to reach and often misses — post-surgical, low confidence, socially isolated, carrying grief and caring responsibilities. What moved her wasn't a programme of exercises. It was a movement she'd practised in play turning up unprompted in her actual life.

    Move Strong – Play Wild and Bushcraft

    From whispering to his mum, to speaking for himself

    When O started, he wouldn't check in. He'd hide behind his mum and speak into her ear, and she'd say it to the group for him.

    Check-in and check-out are how every session opens and closes. Each person says a word for how they're feeling. It's small and it's optional, and for O it was the whole mountain.

    The change came in stages.

    In May, after a session of foam sword work, he spoke for himself for the first time: "I enjoyed the swords and I am happy." The log that week records his brother R planking with ease after months of work, and helping design the games and set the rules — the two brothers moving in the same direction.

    In June, at a bushcraft session, he shared in check-in by himself rather than through his mum. He checked in "excited". A week later, at a shelter-building session, he wanted to check in first.

    In July he took the stick off the facilitator and checked the group out with what he'd enjoyed.

    By September he was checking in with his big brother's support and checking out entirely on his own.

    His mum has told us this is showing up elsewhere — that his confidence in our group is translating into other groups outside the sessions.

    Why this mattersNobody worked on O's speech. He was given a predictable ritual, an invitation he could decline, a group that didn't make a fuss either way, and six months. That's the intervention.

    Nature Moves

    The woman walking her dog

    P wasn't a participant. She was walking her dog past the field on a Monday in August and didn't know what we were doing.

    The facilitator went over and asked.

    She was using walking sticks. She'd had a new hip, and her physiotherapist had told her to find a balance class. She hadn't found one. She joined in that morning on the spot — dog and all.

    It was a swords day: reaction, balance, dodging, duelling. We'd checked in by sharing memorable moments from our lives, and the group had connected quickly — the log from the day records tears, vulnerability and trust in a very short space of time.

    P shared that she'd lost her son the year before. He'd spent his life in a wheelchair but had been active, a sportsman and a traveller. Others in the group could relate — one has a grown-up child who is also a wheelchair user. She was supported, and she said she felt welcomed and a sense of belonging.

    Her arm went higher that day than it ever had since the operation. The reason is in the log: she didn't think about it. She played.

    "I'm nicely surprised by this, it's definitely sparked my interest, thank you."

    P, August

    She said she'd return, and she did. In late August she took part with the help of a stick, resting when she needed to and giving everything a go. On 7 September, during a throwing and catching session, she put her stick down to catch bean bags.

    "I really enjoyed that. Never thought you could do so much with something so simple."

    P, September

    Why this mattersP found us because someone walked over and said hello. No referral, no flyer, no link worker. She'd been told to find a balance class and couldn't find one — while a free one ran in her local park. That's a signposting failure, and it's the single clearest thing we've learnt about reaching the over-50s this year.

    What people told us to improve

    We asked directly, and we're reporting the answers as given.

    Too many games, too many transitions

    One respondent found there were sometimes too many different games with different rules, changing too quickly. Fair, and we've acted on it — later sessions deliberately repeat familiar games, and the logs show the strongest session flow came on days we stayed with games the group already knew.

    Freedom to opt out of the activity

    One parent worried her children were distracting others when they didn't want to join the movement. She was clear she'd never felt pressured, but wanted something else available for them. Worth solving — a parallel free-play option would remove the worry entirely.

    More of it

    By far the most common answer. More sessions, longer sessions, keep it going. One said it can't be improved. Another said: "More beanbags!"

    What we've learnt

    Including the honest account of where we fell short

    Listening changed the programme, and the programme got better for it

    Bushcraft wasn't in the original plan. Families asked for it, we built it in, and it became six of our 26 sessions and some of our strongest moments — the feather sticks session where the mums slowed down and talked to each other while the kids played the field, the shared story in the hammocks where a group of children sat still and fully present for ten minutes, the final feast with bread on the fire.

    The lesson isn't "add bushcraft". It's that some people connect through moving and some connect through making, and a programme that offers only one of those is leaving people out.

    Repetition beats novelty

    Our instinct at the start was to keep sessions fresh. The logs say otherwise.

    The best-flowing sessions were the ones running familiar games — Cat Burglars, Dragon Eggs, the Gauntlet, Sleeping Giants. One facilitator note puts it plainly: good session flow, maybe because they're familiar with these games. A family told us in feedback that too many new games with new rules and quick transitions was the one thing that didn't work.

    Familiarity is what lets people stop concentrating on the instructions and start playing. And playing is where the change happens. We now build sessions around a stable core with one new element, rather than the reverse.

    Slow starts work

    Several logs note a slow start and then a good session. That's not a coincidence. Arriving into a brew, a gazebo or a shelter that's already up, with time to land before anything official begins, consistently produced better engagement than starting on time. We've adjusted — shelter up first, official start slightly later, let everyone arrive.

    Opt-in, opt-out is the reason people stay

    Participants named this themselves. The group told a facilitator in August that they liked how invitational it is — they can opt in and opt out.

    Nearly every significant outcome in this report started with someone watching from the side. The mum who sat out for weeks and then surprised herself. B, who came along with his wife with no intention of joining in, and then did better than he thought. The new girl who watched with her dad and joined when she was ready. O, who checked in through his mum for months. If we'd pushed any of them, we'd have lost them.

    Adults are participants, not supervisors

    The single biggest lever on children's engagement is whether the adults join in. It's in the logs repeatedly: when a parent takes part, their child's engagement rises, and the whole group's tone lifts. We now design for this explicitly — games that work across a three-week-old to a seventy-year-old, and the permission to be visibly bad at them.

    The honest picture on Nature Moves

    Nature Moves has not reached the numbers we wanted. Eight sessions, 21 attendances, an average group of three, and one session with a single participant.

    We've kept running it, and we'd make that choice again. Two of the three case studies in this report are Nature Moves participants, and the depth of outcome in those small groups has been substantial — someone moving from a mobility scooter towards walking her dog, someone putting down a walking stick to catch a bean bag. Small groups allow a level of attention that a class of twenty doesn't.

    But depth doesn't substitute for reach, and we've learnt some specific things about why the numbers are low.

    Referral routes aren't working the way we assumed

    We did the outreach — flyers, GP surgeries, practice managers, social prescribing link workers, council contacts, a mapped circuit of seven surgeries across Shotton, Queensferry, Hawarden and Connah's Quay. Uptake from it has been weak.

    Our clearest success came from someone walking over and saying hello

    P was walking her dog past the session. She'd been told by her physiotherapist to find a balance class and hadn't been able to find one — while a free one was running in her local park. That is the whole problem in one story. The people who need this are being told to look for it, and they can't see it.

    The barriers are psychological before they're practical

    Fear of what "exercise" will involve, reluctance to turn up alone, transport, and plain unfamiliarity with a new thing. The name of the session matters — one participant told us she'd expected an exercise class and found it was much more. Getting people past that expectation is the work.

    What we'd do differently

    Go to where the over-50s already are rather than waiting for referrals — warm hubs, coffee mornings, faith groups, care homes, local Facebook groups. Be present and visible in the park during sessions so passers-by can see what it actually is. Give link workers something concrete enough to hand over. And make it easy to come with someone, because turning up alone is the barrier people name most.

    What we'd ask for next

    More of it, and longer. It's what families asked for more than anything else, and it's what the data supports — the strongest month of the project was August, when the holidays let us run longer, fuller sessions, and September held that gain rather than losing it.

    The family strand is established and growing. The over-50s strand needs a different route to the people it's built for, and that's where the next push goes.
